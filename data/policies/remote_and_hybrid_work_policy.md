# Remote and Hybrid Work Policy (REM-01)

## 1. Purpose, Scope, and Definitions

This policy governs where employees of Northwind Systems Inc. may work. It defines the
default hybrid working model, the conditions under which an employee may work fully
remotely, and the rules that apply when an employee works from a location other than
their assigned office or home location — whether that is another US state or another
country.

Working from another location is **not leave**. An employee working remotely is working
a normal schedule and does not submit a time-off request for those days. Time away from
work is governed by PTO-01.

**Who this policy covers.** All employees of Northwind Systems Inc., at every office
location and in every employment type, including part-time employees. Employees of
Northwind Systems Ltd. (Dublin, Ireland) are covered by this policy for the purpose of
location approvals, with the entity-specific provisions in §6.5.

**Contractors.** Contractors are not employees and are not covered by this policy. A
contractor's working location is governed by the statement of work agreed with the
engaging vendor. A contractor asking for a remote-work or international-work approval
should be routed to the engaging manager and People Operations rather than processed as
a request under this policy.

### 1.1 Definitions

**Assigned office.** The Northwind office recorded against an employee in the HR system.
Northwind operates offices in Austin, TX; San Francisco, CA; and New York, NY, and an
international entity in Dublin, Ireland.

**Home location.** The address of record from which a remote or hybrid employee
ordinarily works. This is the location Northwind treats as the employee's normal place
of work for the purposes of this policy and of TAX-01.

**Hybrid.** The default working model, described in §2: a mix of in-office and remote
working, applicable to employees who live within commuting distance of an office.

**Fully remote.** A status, described in §3, in which an employee has no in-office
attendance requirement. It is granted by exception and is recorded in the HR system.

**Temporary work from another location.** Working from somewhere other than the home
location for a defined period, with the intention of returning. This is what §4 and §5
govern. It is distinct from a permanent change of home location, which is handled by
People Operations outside this policy.

**Business day.** A day on which Northwind's US offices are scheduled to be open,
excluding weekends and company holidays.

**Calendar day.** Every day including weekends and holidays. The distinction matters:
domestic limits in §4 are counted in business days, and international limits in §5 are
counted in calendar days.

### 1.2 How to use this document

The hybrid default is in §2 and fully remote status is in §3. Domestic out-of-state work
is in §4. International work is in §5, and the approved country list is in §6. Notice
periods and the request process are in §7. Core hours are in §8. Security obligations
are in §9. Interactions with other policies are in §10. Worked examples are in §11 and
frequently asked questions in §12.

### 1.3 Related documents

- TAX-01 — work location and tax compliance. Any question about tax consequences,
  registration, or permanent establishment is answered there, not here.
- SEC-01 — data security and acceptable use, including the rules for devices, networks,
  and data handling while working away from an office.
- EQP-01 — equipment and assets, including what hardware is provided and what happens
  to it when an employee works from another location.
- PTO-01 — paid time off. Leave and remote work are separate requests; see §10.1.
- BEN-01 — benefits, including the separate Irish benefits schedule.
- APR-01 — the consolidated approvals and escalation matrix, which restates every
  approval threshold in this policy alongside those from other documents.
- CON-01 — workplace conduct and grievance, including the route for raising a concern
  about how a location request was handled.

## 2. The Hybrid Default

Northwind's default working model is **hybrid**. Employees whose home location is
**within 50 miles of a Northwind office** are expected to work from that office **2 days
per week**.

### 2.1 Who the hybrid expectation applies to

The 50-mile radius is measured from the employee's home location of record to their
assigned office. An employee inside that radius is a hybrid employee by default and does
not need to apply for anything; the expectation applies automatically.

An employee whose home location is more than 50 miles from any Northwind office has no
in-office attendance expectation under this section. They are not automatically classed
as fully remote for the purposes of §3 — fully remote is a recorded status, not a
consequence of distance — but in practice they work remotely and the 2-day expectation
does not apply to them.

### 2.2 Which days

This policy does not fix which 2 days per week are worked in the office. Teams choose
their own in-office days so that the people who need to collaborate are present at the
same time, and managers are responsible for setting and communicating that pattern.
Employees should expect their team's pattern to be consistent rather than negotiated
week by week.

### 2.3 Weeks when the expectation cannot be met

The expectation is a weekly average obligation, not a rule that overrides everything
else. An employee who is on approved leave, unwell, travelling on business, or working
from another location under §4 or §5 is not expected to make up missed office days
afterwards.

Where an employee repeatedly cannot meet the expectation for reasons that are not
covered above, the manager should discuss it with them and, if the situation is
ongoing, consider whether a fully remote exception under §3 is the honest description
of the arrangement.

### 2.4 Accessibility and individual circumstances

An employee who cannot meet the in-office expectation for reasons related to disability,
health, or caring responsibilities should raise it with their manager or People
Operations. Northwind will consider adjustments to the pattern, including reduced or
zero in-office attendance, without requiring the employee to disclose more detail than
they are comfortable giving. An adjustment agreed on this basis is recorded by People
Operations and is not treated as a fully remote exception under §3.

### 2.5 What the office is for

In-office days exist for collaboration, onboarding, and the kind of unplanned
conversation that does not happen over chat. Managers should schedule team meetings,
planning sessions, and new-hire support on in-office days where practical, so that the
expectation delivers something and is not simply attendance for its own sake.

## 3. Fully Remote Status

Fully remote status is granted **by exception and requires VP approval**. It removes the
in-office expectation in §2 entirely and is recorded against the employee in the HR
system.

### 3.1 When fully remote status is appropriate

Fully remote status is appropriate where an employee's role can be performed without
regular physical presence, where the employee's home location makes in-office work
impractical, or where a specific individual circumstance justifies it. It is a statement
about the working arrangement, not a reward or a seniority marker.

### 3.2 Requesting fully remote status

A request is raised with the direct manager, who forwards it to the VP of the employee's
function with a recommendation. The VP approves or declines, and People Operations
records the outcome. A fully remote arrangement that has not been recorded in the HR
system does not exist for the purposes of this policy, however long it has been the
informal practice.

The approving VP should consider whether the role genuinely works without regular
presence, whether the team can operate with the employee remote, and whether the
employee's home location raises the tax or registration questions covered by TAX-01.

### 3.3 What fully remote status does and does not change

Fully remote status removes the in-office expectation. It does not change any other
obligation in this policy. A fully remote employee is still bound by the core hours in
§8, still needs approval to work from another state under §4 or another country under
§5, and is still bound by the security obligations in §9.

In particular, fully remote status is **not** permission to work from anywhere. The
employee's home location remains their recorded place of work, and working from
somewhere else is still a temporary arrangement subject to §4 or §5.

### 3.4 Changing or ending fully remote status

An employee may ask to return to hybrid working at any time, and the change is recorded
by People Operations. Northwind may also end a fully remote arrangement where the role
changes materially or where the arrangement is no longer working, with reasonable notice
and a discussion with the employee first.

Where an employee moves home while fully remote, they must report the change under the
reporting obligation in TAX-01, because a change of home location can carry tax and
registration consequences that the employee cannot assess alone.

## 4. Working from Another US State

An employee may work temporarily from a US state other than their home location, subject
to the duration limits in this section. The limits are counted in **consecutive business
days** in a single block.

| Duration | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Up to 10 consecutive business days | No approval required |
| 11–30 consecutive business days | Manager approval required |
| More than 30 consecutive business days | HR and Tax review required, see TAX-01 |

### 4.1 Up to 10 business days

A block of up to 10 consecutive business days needs no approval. The employee should
tell their manager where they will be, so the team knows, and should confirm that they
can meet the core hours in §8 from that location. No form is required and no record is
created beyond the employee's own calendar.

This tier exists so that ordinary life — visiting family, a change of scene, a partner's
work trip — does not generate paperwork.

### 4.2 Eleven to 30 business days

A block of 11 to 30 consecutive business days requires the direct manager's approval in
advance. The manager is assessing whether the work can be done effectively from that
location and during core hours, not whether the reason is good enough.

### 4.3 More than 30 business days

A block of more than 30 consecutive business days requires review by both HR and the Tax
function before it begins. This is not a formality and it is not a higher tier of manager
approval: working from a state for an extended period can create registration and
withholding obligations for Northwind, and the thresholds that govern that are defined in
TAX-01.

Employees should assume that a request in this tier takes time to assess and should raise
it as early as possible. Where the review concludes that the location is not workable,
the alternatives are to shorten the stay to fit a lower tier, to choose a different
location, or — where the employee wants to move permanently — to discuss a change of home
location with People Operations.

### 4.4 Counting the duration

The tier is set by the length of the block, not by how many days the employee happens to
work. Weekends and company holidays inside a block do not count as business days, but
they do not break the block either.

Consecutive blocks separated by a short return home are still assessed as a pattern.
Repeatedly taking 10-business-day trips to the same state, back to back, is a long stay
split into pieces, and both the manager and the employee should treat it as one. If in
doubt, raise it with People Operations rather than resetting the count.

### 4.5 Working from an office other than the assigned one

Working from a different Northwind office is not a temporary arrangement under this
section and needs no approval, whatever its length. The employee should book a desk
through the usual channel and tell their manager.

## 5. Working Internationally

International work is subject to tighter limits than domestic work, and the limits here
are counted in **calendar days**, not business days.

**Working internationally without approval is never permitted.** There is no equivalent
of the no-approval tier in §4.1. Every international working arrangement, of any length,
requires approval before it begins.

| Duration | Requirement |
|---|---|
| 14 calendar days or fewer | Manager approval and HR notification, plus Security review |
| 15–30 calendar days | Manager, HR, and Tax approval |
| More than 30 calendar days | **Prohibited**, unless the employee transfers to a local entity |

All international requests are additionally subject to the country restrictions in §6
and the minimum notice period in §7.1.

### 5.1 Fourteen calendar days or fewer

The shortest tier still requires the direct manager's approval and notification to HR,
and it requires a Security review before travel. The Security review covers the device
the employee will carry and the networks they expect to use; the substantive rules are
in SEC-01, and Security may require a different device arrangement as a condition of
approval.

### 5.2 Fifteen to 30 calendar days

A stay of 15 to 30 calendar days requires approval from the manager, HR, and the Tax
function — three approvals, all recorded before travel. The Tax review exists because a
stay of this length starts to raise questions about where the employee is working for
tax purposes, and those questions are assessed against TAX-01.

### 5.3 More than 30 calendar days

International work of more than 30 calendar days is **prohibited**. It cannot be approved
by a manager, by HR, by the Tax function, or by a VP, and there is no exception process
for it within this policy.

The only route to working from another country for longer than 30 calendar days is a
**transfer to a local Northwind entity**, which is a change of employment rather than a
travel arrangement. A transfer requires an entity to exist in that country, and it
changes the employee's contract, payroll, benefits, and statutory entitlements. Employees
who want to explore a transfer should raise it with People Operations; it is a process
measured in months, not a request that can be approved before a planned trip.

This prohibition is the single most misunderstood rule in this policy, so it is worth
stating plainly: a long stay abroad cannot be authorised by splitting it into shorter
approved blocks, by taking leave for part of it, or by describing part of it as a
holiday. See §10.1.

### 5.4 Why the international limits are stricter

The limits above are not about trust. A person working from another country can create
obligations for Northwind in that country — tax, employment, and data protection
obligations — that arise from their presence rather than from what they are doing.
Permanent establishment risk is assessed under TAX-01. The practical effect is that
Northwind can accommodate short international stays readily and cannot accommodate long
ones at all without an entity in place.

## 6. Approved Countries

International work is permitted **only in Ireland, Canada, and the United Kingdom**.

These three are the only approved countries because Northwind either operates an entity
there or has treaty coverage that makes a short stay assessable. No other country is
approved, and a country's absence from the list is a decision rather than an oversight.

### 6.1 Requests for countries not on the list

A request to work from a country that is not Ireland, Canada, or the United Kingdom
cannot be approved, regardless of duration, regardless of how much notice is given, and
regardless of who supports it. There is no VP override and no exception route.

This is a hard restriction rather than a preference, and managers should decline such
requests directly rather than forwarding them for a decision that cannot be made. When
declining, the manager should set out what *is* available: an approved country, a
domestic location under §4, or time off under PTO-01 taken as leave rather than as work.

### 6.2 The list is not a statement about the country

The approved list reflects where Northwind has an entity or treaty coverage. It says
nothing about the country an employee wants to work from, and an employee should not read
a refusal as a judgement about their destination, their family, or their reasons.

### 6.3 Changes to the list

The approved country list changes only when Northwind's entity footprint or treaty
coverage changes. Employees who believe a country should be added should raise it with
People Operations, but should plan on the basis of the list as it stands.

### 6.4 Travel that is not work

Nothing in this policy restricts where an employee spends their own time. An employee may
take PTO anywhere in the world. The restrictions here apply to *working* — attending
meetings, answering messages, writing code, accessing Northwind systems — from that
location. The distinction is the activity, not the passport stamp, and it is the reason
§10.1 treats a trip that mixes leave and work as two separate requests.

### 6.5 Employees of the Irish entity

Employees of Northwind Systems Ltd. in Dublin are subject to the same structure in
reverse: working from the United States or Canada is an international arrangement for
them and follows the tiers in §5, with the same notice period and the same prohibition
beyond 30 calendar days. Their approvals route through the EMEA leadership chain and HR
rather than through the US chain.

## 7. Notice, Requests, and Approvals

### 7.1 Minimum notice for international requests

Every international request requires a **minimum of 30 calendar days' notice**, measured
from the date the request is submitted to the first day of working from that country.

The notice period applies to every international tier, including the shortest. A
three-day trip to Ireland needs the same 30 calendar days' notice as a four-week one,
because the notice exists to allow the Security review in §5.1 and, where applicable, the
Tax review in §5.2 to happen properly.

A request submitted with less than 30 calendar days' notice cannot be approved. The
employee's options are to move the dates so the notice period is met, or to take the time
as leave under PTO-01 and not work during it.

### 7.2 Notice for domestic requests

Domestic requests under §4 have no fixed notice period. An employee should give their
manager enough warning to plan, and a request in the 11–30 business day tier should be
raised well before travel is booked. A request in the over-30-day tier should be raised
as early as possible because of the HR and Tax review in §4.3.

### 7.3 How to submit a request

Location requests are submitted in the HR portal with the destination, the exact start
and end dates, and — for international requests — the intended working pattern. The
portal routes the request to the approvers required by the relevant tier. A request is
approved only when every required approval is recorded; a manager's approval alone does
not satisfy a tier that also requires HR, Tax, or Security.

Employees should not book non-refundable travel before the approvals are recorded.

### 7.4 What approvers assess

An approver should confirm, in order:

1. That the destination is permitted — for international requests, that it is Ireland,
   Canada, or the United Kingdom, per §6.
2. That the duration falls in a tier that can be approved at all, per §4 and §5.
3. That the notice period is met, per §7.1.
4. That the employee can work the core hours in §8 from that location.
5. That the work can be done effectively from there.

Only the last is a judgement call. The first three are determined by this policy, and an
approver has no discretion to waive them.

### 7.5 Changes after approval

An approval covers the destination and dates requested. Extending a stay is a new
request, assessed at the tier matching the **total** length, not the extension alone. An
employee who extends an international stay past 30 calendar days without a new approval
is in breach of §5.3, and the fact that the original block was approved does not cure it.

Cancelling or shortening an approved arrangement needs no approval; the employee should
tell their manager and update the record in the HR portal.

## 8. Core Hours and Availability

All employees, wherever they are working, are expected to be available during **core
overlap hours of 10:00 to 15:00 US Central**, regardless of location.

### 8.1 Why core hours are anchored to one timezone

A shared overlap window is what makes a distributed team workable. Anchoring it to a
single timezone rather than to each employee's local time means the window is the same
window for everybody, which is the entire point: a meeting that suits everyone's local
morning suits nobody's calendar.

### 8.2 What availability means

Availability during core hours means being reachable and able to join a conversation. It
does not mean being at a desk continuously, and it does not mean core hours are the only
hours worked. Outside the window, employees and teams arrange their own schedules.

### 8.3 Core hours and location requests

Core hours are one of the things an approver assesses under §7.4. A location whose local
time makes the core window fall in the middle of the night is a practical obstacle to
approval, even when the destination and duration are otherwise permitted. Employees
should work out the overlap before requesting, and should say in the request how they
intend to cover it.

### 8.4 Core hours and leave

Core hours apply to working days. An employee on approved leave under PTO-01 is not
expected to be available during core hours, or at any other time.

### 8.5 Adjustments

A team or an individual may agree a different overlap arrangement with their manager
where the work genuinely does not require the standard window. The arrangement should be
written down and shared with the team, and People Operations should be told, so that the
exception is visible rather than folk knowledge.

## 9. Security Obligations While Working Remotely

Working outside an office does not relax any security obligation. The substantive rules —
device standards, network requirements, data classification, and incident reporting — are
defined in SEC-01, and this section only identifies where they bite hardest for remote
and travelling employees.

### 9.1 Devices

Employees work on company-provided equipment, provisioned under EQP-01. The rules on
personal devices, and the enrolment those devices require, are in SEC-01.

Travel to a country that Security classifies as high-risk requires a loaner device issued
by the Security team rather than the employee's usual laptop. The classification and the
loaner process are defined in SEC-01; the practical consequence for this policy is that
such travel must be raised early enough for a device to be issued, which is one of the
reasons for the notice period in §7.1.

### 9.2 Networks

Access to Northwind systems from public or untrusted networks is subject to the
requirements in SEC-01. Employees working from hotels, cafés, airports, and shared
workspaces should read that document before travelling rather than after.

### 9.3 Data location

Some categories of Northwind data may not be accessed from outside defined regions at
all. SEC-01 defines the data classes and the geographic restrictions on each. An employee
whose role involves the most sensitive categories should confirm with Security, before
requesting an international arrangement, that they will be able to do their job from the
destination — a location approval under §5 is not a data-access approval.

### 9.4 Incidents while travelling

A lost or stolen device, or any suspected security incident, is reported under the
timelines in SEC-01 and EQP-01. Being in another timezone does not extend those
timelines. Employees travelling should know how to reach Security before they leave.

## 10. Interaction with Leave, Tax, and Benefits

### 10.1 Leave (PTO-01)

Working remotely is not leave, and leave is not working remotely. The two are separate
requests with separate approvers and separate rules.

An employee who wants to take PTO in another country and then work from that country for
the rest of the trip is making two requests: a PTO request under PTO-01 for the days off,
and an international work request under this policy for the working days. Neither
approval implies the other.

Two consequences follow, and both matter:

- **Approved PTO does not authorise working from that location.** An employee on leave
  abroad who starts working is working internationally without approval, which §5
  prohibits outright.
- **Leave cannot be used to get around §5.3.** A stay of more than 30 calendar days
  cannot be made permissible by classifying part of it as leave. The prohibition is on
  working in the country beyond that limit; time off inside a longer trip does not reset
  or offset it.

### 10.2 Tax and work location (TAX-01)

This policy decides whether an employee **may** work from a location. TAX-01 decides what
follows for tax and registration if they do, and it carries its own obligation on
employees to report location changes. The reviews referenced in §4.3 and §5.2 are
conducted against TAX-01's thresholds.

Employees should not attempt to assess their own tax position, and should not rely on
what a colleague was allowed to do previously: the registration position varies by state
and by country and changes over time.

### 10.3 Benefits (BEN-01)

Working temporarily from another location does not change an employee's benefits
elections or eligibility. A transfer to a local entity under §5.3 does change them,
because the employee moves onto that entity's benefits schedule; the Irish schedule is
described in BEN-01.

### 10.4 Equipment (EQP-01)

Equipment provided for use at the home location may be taken to another location. Bulky
items are not shipped for temporary arrangements. Replacement equipment while travelling,
and the treatment of loss or damage, are covered by EQP-01.

### 10.5 Conduct (CON-01)

Every conduct obligation applies identically to remote work. An employee who believes a
location request has been handled unfairly, or who wishes to raise any other concern, has
the channels set out in CON-01.

## 11. Worked Examples

These examples show how the rules combine. Each states the facts, the rules that apply,
and the outcome.

### 11.1 A month in Spain

An employee asks to work from Spain for six weeks, starting in three weeks' time.

Three separate rules each block this request independently. Spain is not Ireland, Canada,
or the United Kingdom, so §6 rules it out on destination alone. Six weeks exceeds 30
calendar days, so §5.3 prohibits it on duration regardless of destination. Three weeks'
notice is short of the 30 calendar days required by §7.1.

**Outcome:** declined, and it cannot be escalated — there is no approver who can grant
any of the three. The manager should decline it directly and set out the alternatives:
an equivalent stay in an approved country, subject to the duration tiers and notice; a
domestic arrangement under §4; taking the time as leave under PTO-01 without working; or,
if the employee wants to live in Spain, a conversation with People Operations about
whether a local entity exists, knowing that no entity means no route.

### 11.2 Two weeks in Ireland

An employee asks to work from Dublin for 12 calendar days, submitting the request 45
calendar days beforehand.

The destination is approved under §6. The duration falls in the 14-calendar-day tier, so
§5.1 requires manager approval, HR notification, and a Security review. Notice exceeds
the 30 calendar days required by §7.1. Core hours are workable with an early finish.

**Outcome:** approvable. It is not approved until the manager's approval, the HR
notification, and the Security review are all recorded, and the employee should not book
non-refundable travel before then.

### 11.3 Three weeks in Canada

An employee asks to work from Vancouver for 21 calendar days with two months' notice.

Canada is approved. Twenty-one calendar days falls in the 15–30 day tier, so §5.2
requires manager, HR, **and** Tax approval — three approvals, not two.

**Outcome:** approvable, subject to all three approvals plus the Security review. The Tax
review may take time; the notice is generous enough to accommodate it.

### 11.4 A long domestic stay

An employee based in Austin asks to work from a state where Northwind is not registered,
for 38 consecutive business days.

Thirty-eight business days exceeds the 30-day threshold in §4, so §4.3 requires HR and Tax
review. This is a domestic request, so §5's international prohibition does not apply and
the length is not automatically fatal.

**Outcome:** reviewable, not automatically approved or declined. The review is conducted
against TAX-01's thresholds. If the outcome is negative, the employee may shorten the
stay into a lower tier or choose a different state.

### 11.5 A week away, no approval needed

An employee wants to work from another US state for three business days.

This falls in §4.1. No approval is required.

**Outcome:** proceed. Tell the manager where you will be and confirm you can cover core
hours from there. No form, no record, no approval.

### 11.6 Extending a trip

An employee approved for 20 calendar days in the United Kingdom asks, while there, to
stay another 15.

Under §7.5, the extension is assessed at the total length — 35 calendar days — not at the
15 days being added. Thirty-five calendar days exceeds the limit in §5.3.

**Outcome:** the extension is prohibited. The original approval does not cure it, and the
employee must return at the end of the approved period or stop working for the remainder
of the stay and take it as leave, if their manager approves the leave under PTO-01.

### 11.7 Fully remote, then travelling

A fully remote employee assumes that fully remote status lets them work from anywhere.

**Outcome:** it does not, per §3.3. Their home location remains their recorded place of
work. Working from another state is assessed under §4 and from another country under §5,
on exactly the same terms as for a hybrid employee.

## 12. Frequently Asked Questions

**How many days a week do I need to be in the office?**
Two, if your home location is within 50 miles of a Northwind office. See §2.

**How is the 50 miles measured?**
From your home location of record to your assigned office. See §2.1.

**Can I work from another US state without asking?**
Yes, for up to 10 consecutive business days. Beyond that you need manager approval, and
beyond 30 business days you need HR and Tax review. See §4.

**Can I work from another country without asking?**
No. International work always requires approval, at every length. See §5.

**Which countries can I work from?**
Ireland, Canada, and the United Kingdom only. See §6.

**How much notice do I need for an international request?**
30 calendar days, for every international tier including the shortest. See §7.1.

**Can I work abroad for two months if I split it into approved blocks?**
No. The limit is on working in the country, and splitting a long stay does not reset it.
See §5.3 and §7.5.

**I'm taking PTO abroad and want to work for part of the trip. Is that one request?**
No, two — a PTO request and an international work request. See §10.1.

**I'm fully remote. Do these rules still apply to me?**
Yes, all of them except the in-office expectation. See §3.3.

**What are core hours?**
10:00 to 15:00 US Central, wherever you are. See §8.

**Can my VP approve a country that isn't on the list?**
No. There is no override. See §6.1.

**I'm a contractor. Does this policy apply to me?**
No. Your working location is governed by your statement of work. See §1.

## 13. Roles, Responsibilities, and Document Control

### 13.1 Employees

Employees are responsible for knowing which tier their request falls into before making
plans, for submitting requests with the notice required by §7, for not booking
non-refundable travel before approvals are recorded, for covering core hours from
wherever they work, for reporting location changes as TAX-01 requires, and for meeting
every SEC-01 obligation while away.

### 13.2 Managers

Managers approve domestic requests in the 11–30 business day tier and are an approver on
every international request. They are responsible for applying the tests in §7.4 in
order, for declining requests that no approver can grant rather than forwarding them, for
setting and communicating the team's in-office pattern, and for raising repeated
short-trip patterns rather than treating each in isolation.

### 13.3 VPs

VPs approve fully remote status under §3. They have no authority to approve a country
outside §6 or a stay beyond the limit in §5.3.

### 13.4 HR and Tax

HR is an approver or notified party on every international request and, with Tax,
reviews domestic stays beyond 30 business days and international stays of 15 to 30
calendar days. Tax reviews are conducted against TAX-01.

### 13.5 Security

Security reviews every international request and issues loaner devices for travel to
high-risk countries, under SEC-01.

### 13.6 People Operations

People Operations owns this policy, records fully remote status and accessibility
adjustments, handles permanent changes of home location and entity transfers, and
maintains the approved country list.

### 13.7 Raising a concern

An employee who believes a location request was handled unfairly should raise it with
their manager in the first instance, and may raise it with their HR Business Partner or
through the anonymous ethics line. The channels, timelines, and anti-retaliation
protections are set out in CON-01.

### 13.8 Out of scope

This policy covers where employees may work. It does not cover time away from work, tax
consequences, security requirements, or equipment provision, each of which has its own
document. Where an employee asks about a working arrangement that is not described here
or in another Northwind policy, the correct answer is that the policy library does not
cover it and the question should go to People Operations.

### 13.9 Version and review

This is version v4.2, effective 2026-01-01. It supersedes all prior versions of the
Northwind Remote and Hybrid Work Policy. The policy is owned by People Operations and is
reviewed annually. A consolidated view of every approval threshold in this policy,
alongside those from the other Northwind policy documents, is maintained in APR-01.
